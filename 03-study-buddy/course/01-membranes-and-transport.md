# Lecture 1: Membranes and transport

*Introductory Cell Biology (BIO 110), synthetic course notes written for this demo.*

## The plasma membrane

Every cell is wrapped in a plasma membrane about 7–8 nanometres thick. Its backbone is a
phospholipid bilayer: each phospholipid has a hydrophilic phosphate head that faces the watery
inside or outside of the cell, and two hydrophobic fatty-acid tails that point into the middle
of the bilayer. Cholesterol sits between the tails and keeps the membrane from becoming too
fluid when warm or too stiff when cold. Proteins float in the bilayer like boats on a lake,
which is why the structure is called the **fluid mosaic model**. Integral proteins span the
whole bilayer; peripheral proteins sit on one face. Short sugar chains attached to proteins and
lipids on the outer face (glycoproteins and glycolipids) let cells recognise one another.

The membrane is **selectively permeable**. Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon
dioxide slip straight through the bilayer. Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose
cannot cross the hydrophobic core on their own and need a protein.

## Passive transport: no ATP

**Simple diffusion** is the net movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to
a region of lower concentration, straight through the bilayer. Oxygen entering a red blood cell
is the classic example.

**Facilitated diffusion** also moves molecules down their concentration gradient and also uses
no ATP, but the molecules pass through a channel protein or are carried by a carrier protein.
Glucose entering most body cells through GLUT transporters is facilitated diffusion.
Aquaporins are channel proteins for water.

**Osmosis** is the diffusion of *water* across a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves
toward the side with the higher solute concentration, that is, toward the side with less free
water. Put a red blood cell in a **hypotonic** solution (fewer solutes outside) and water
rushes in until the cell may burst (lysis). In a **hypertonic** solution (more solutes outside)
water leaves and the cell shrivels (crenation). In an **isotonic** solution there is no net
movement. Plant cells in hypotonic water become turgid rather than bursting, because the cell
wall pushes back.

## Active transport: uses ATP

**Active transport** moves substances *against* their concentration gradient, from low to high
concentration, and it always needs energy, usually from ATP. The best-known example is the
**sodium–potassium pump** (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase): for each ATP it hydrolyses, it pumps **3 sodium
ions out** of the cell and **2 potassium ions in**. This keeps the inside of the cell negative
relative to the outside, which nerve and muscle cells rely on.

## Bulk transport

Large particles cross in vesicles. **Endocytosis** brings material *into* the cell: phagocytosis
("cell eating") engulfs large particles such as bacteria, and pinocytosis ("cell drinking") takes
in droplets of fluid. **Exocytosis** releases material *out of* the cell when a vesicle fuses
with the plasma membrane, which is how neurons release neurotransmitters and how pancreatic
cells secrete insulin. Both forms of bulk transport use energy.

## Common exam traps

- Facilitated diffusion uses a protein but no ATP; it is passive.
- Osmosis is about water moving, not solutes moving.
- The pump ratio is 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, not the other way round.
